Gross Motor Pod
Drawing a Person (3+ Parts)
FMS: Drawing a Person
▾
Why This Matters

Drawing a person with recognisable, distinct body parts is a well-established fine-motor and body-schema milestone. At 4, most children can include at least a head, eyes, and body — this week explicitly tracks progress toward more parts (arms, legs, hair) over the coming weeks.

🏃 FMS Technique Cues — Drawing a Person (3+ Parts)
Start with the head — it's usually easiest
Add eyes, then other face features
Add the body, then arms and legs
There's no wrong way to draw a person at this age

FMS evidence & branding note →

🔄 Locomotor Warm-Up

Walk backward, 4 steps (consolidating).

Activities
  • Draw a person (EYLF 4): "Draw a picture of a person — as many parts as you can." No demonstration given, so it reflects the child's own body awareness.
  • Balance challenge (EYLF 3): Stand on the preferred foot for 1 second, eyes closed.
  • Locomotor warm-up (EYLF 3): Walk backward, 4 steps (consolidating).

VariationVary the activity to keep engagement high while practising the same underlying skill.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Never demonstrate or draw an example before the child attempts their own — this is a genuine assessment of their own body-schema knowledge, and a model changes what you're seeing.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent worries about pace: "Every child develops these skills at a different rate — what matters is regular, playful practice."

⚠️ Common Parent Trap

It's tempting to prompt "don't forget the arms!" — but naming missing parts changes the task from a body-schema check into a memory-and-instruction-following task. Let the drawing be entirely the child's own.

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: attempting a genuinely hard physical skill in front of peers, even imperfectly, is an act of initiative worth naming and celebrating.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Effort Over Mastery

Parents celebrate the attempt at a hard skill, not just a perfect result.

🏠 Try This at Home

Let them draw their own way before showing them how — it's their own body knowledge, not a copying task.

Complex 3-Step Instructions in Play
▾
Why This Matters

Following a three-step instruction embedded naturally within play (rather than as an isolated instruction-following drill) tests whether the skill genuinely transfers to real activity, which is the ultimate goal.

Activities
  • Embedded instructions in a game (EYLF 5): "Get the red block, put it on the blue one, then knock it down" — within the flow of play rather than as a standalone task.
  • Spatial concept — forward/backward (EYLF 5): Movement games naming the direction as it happens.
  • Object use — house, aeroplane, clock (EYLF 5): "What do we do with a clock?"

VariationBring the target language into an existing favourite activity rather than treating it as a stand-alone drill.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

If a child completes only the first or last step, that's a common pattern (primacy/recency) — repeat the full instruction once, rather than just the missed step, so they practise holding the whole sequence.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent answers for the child: "Try waiting five seconds before stepping in — the pause does more work than a prompt."

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: offering language, even imperfectly, is a small act of communicative initiative — respond warmly to the attempt.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Model, Then Wait

Parents offer the language model, then give real waiting time before jumping in.

Cognitive Pod
Sorting by Two Attributes
Pre-Literacy: Point to the remaining letters
▾
Why This Matters

Sorting objects by two attributes at once (big AND red, not just big or just red) is a genuine step up in classification complexity — most 4-year-olds can sort by one attribute reliably, and two attributes is the appropriate stretch.

🧠 Pre-Literacy / Numeracy Threads This Week

Pre-Literacy: Point to the remaining letters + Numeracy: Comparing quantities (larger sets)

Activities
  • Two-attribute sort (EYLF 4): A mixed set of shapes/colours/sizes, sorted into groups that share two features.
  • Odd-one-out (EYLF 4): Four items, one different by shape or letter — which doesn't belong?
  • Pre-literacy — Point to the remaining letters (EYLF 5).
  • Numeracy — Comparing quantities (larger sets) (EYLF 4).

VariationTurn the core activity into a short game with a silly or novel twist to keep motivation high.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

If two-attribute sorting is too hard, drop back to one attribute at a time and build up — this isn't a skill to push through frustration on.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

On persistence: if a child loses interest partway through a tricky cognitive task, invite one more playful attempt rather than letting the first try be the only one.

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: guessing or attempting an answer in front of others carries a small social risk — a relaxed tone protects a child's willingness to keep trying.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Support the Thinking, Not the Answer

Parents make the task slightly easier when needed, rather than supplying the answer directly.

Social-Emotional Pod
Empathy — Noticing and Comforting a Peer
SEL: Social Awareness
▾
Why This Matters

Noticing when a peer is upset and offering comfort (a hug, a kind word, fetching an adult) is a genuine empathic skill emerging around this age — distinct from simply reacting to one's own emotions, which the program has focused on until now.

Activities
  • Noticing feelings in others (EYLF 2): During group time, gently point out and name a peer's visible emotion — "look, Mia looks a bit sad, I wonder why."
  • Comfort role-play (EYLF 2): Practise with a doll or puppet — "the puppet is sad, what could we do?"

VariationLook for a natural moment during free play where this week's skill genuinely comes up, rather than only practising it in a set activity.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Model the comfort behaviour yourself first — a hand on the shoulder, a kind word — before asking a child to initiate it themselves.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

Model the target behaviour yourself first — children copy what they see far more readily than what they're told.

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: this term has tracked a steady shift from needing support (Autonomy) toward attempting things independently in a social context (Initiative) — this week's skill sits squarely in that shift.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Notice and Name It

Parents notice and warmly name the target behaviour when it happens, rather than only correcting when it doesn't.

🏠 Try This at Home

Big AND red, not just one or the other.

Self-Help Pod
Table Manners and Mealtime Routine
ADL: Mealtime Independence
▾
Why This Matters

Basic table manners — staying seated, using "please" and "thank you," waiting until everyone is served — are social-adaptive skills that support smooth family and school mealtimes, distinct from the cutlery mechanics already covered.

Activities
  • Shared snack routine (EYLF 2): A structured group snack time practising the whole routine — sitting, waiting, asking, and thanking.
  • Role modelling (EYLF 2): Adults modelling the exact language and behaviour expected, narrated as it happens.

VariationBuild the practice into a real daily routine rather than a stand-alone drill — the transfer to real life matters most.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Keep expectations realistic for a group setting — a few consistent, achievable habits (asking, saying thank you) matter more than a long list of rules at once.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent steps in too quickly: "Give it a little longer before helping — the attempt itself is the practice."

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: "I can do it myself" is the developmental hallmark of this stage — even when it's slower or messier than an adult stepping in.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Let Them Struggle Before You Help

Parents wait through a genuine struggle before stepping in, rather than helping out of habit.

🏠 Try This at Home

Sitting, waiting, asking, and saying thank you — a few consistent habits matter more than a long list.
