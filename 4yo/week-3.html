Week 3

Week 3 builds on the term's progression: catching two-handed, greater distance in motor, following three-step directions in communication, syllable clapping in cognitive development, turn-taking in a rule-based game in social-emotional growth, and managing buttons and zips in self-help. This week also continues the balance, locomotor warm-up, and letter/numeral threads running across the whole term.

Catching Two-Handed, Greater Distance
FMS: Catching — Greater Distance
▾
Why This Matters

Catching is developmentally later than throwing, and by 4, children are ready to catch from further away and with a smaller margin for error than the close, large-ball catching practised earlier.

🏃 FMS Technique Cues — Catching Two-Handed, Greater Distance
Hands out in front, ready
Eyes on the ball the whole way
Catch with both hands, then bring it to your chest
Soft hands — don't fight the ball

FMS evidence & branding note →

⚖️ Balance Challenge This Week

Stand on the other foot for 1 second.

🔄 Locomotor Warm-Up

Heel-to-toe forward, 3 steps.

Activities
  • Catch and step back (EYLF 3): Successful catches earn a step further away, gradually increasing distance.
  • Balloon-to-ball transition (EYLF 3): Start with a slow balloon, moving to a soft ball as confidence builds.
  • Balance challenge (EYLF 3): Stand on the other foot for 1 second.
  • Locomotor warm-up (EYLF 3): Heel-to-toe forward, 3 steps.

VariationVary the activity to keep engagement high while practising the same underlying skill.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

If a child flinches or turns away from the ball, go back to the balloon — the slower speed removes the fear response and lets the technique be practised safely.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent worries about pace: "Every child develops these skills at a different rate — what matters is regular, playful practice."

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: attempting a genuinely hard physical skill in front of peers, even imperfectly, is an act of initiative worth naming and celebrating.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Effort Over Mastery

Parents celebrate the attempt at a hard skill, not just a perfect result.

🏠 Try This at Home

Start close with a soft ball, then take a step back each time they catch it.

Following Three-Step Directions
▾
Why This Matters

Following a three-step direction ("get your bag, put your shoes on, then come sit down") in order and without repetition is a genuine school-readiness benchmark, building on the one- and two-step directions already established.

Activities
  • Three-step instruction games (EYLF 5): "Touch your head, then clap twice, then sit down" — increase to three steps only once two-step is reliable.
  • Spatial concept — up/down (EYLF 5): Movement games naming up and down as they happen.
  • Body parts — knees, thumbs, fingernails (EYLF 5): Simon Says or a body-part song.

VariationBring the target language into an existing favourite activity rather than treating it as a stand-alone drill.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

If three steps consistently overwhelm a child, break it into two steps plus a pause, gradually removing the pause rather than the step.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent answers for the child: "Try waiting five seconds before stepping in — the pause does more work than a prompt."

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: offering language, even imperfectly, is a small act of communicative initiative — respond warmly to the attempt.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Model, Then Wait

Parents offer the language model, then give real waiting time before jumping in.

Syllable Clapping
Pre-Literacy: Name 5 uppercase letters
▾
Why This Matters

Clapping out the syllables in a word (wa-ter, ba-na-na) is a core phonological-awareness skill that sits alongside rhyming as a strong predictor of later reading success.

🧠 Pre-Literacy / Numeracy Threads This Week

Pre-Literacy: Name 5 uppercase letters + Numeracy: Comparing quantities — which has more

Activities
  • Clap the syllables (EYLF 5): Say a word slowly, clapping once per syllable, starting with the child's own name.
  • Pre-literacy — Name 5 uppercase letters (EYLF 5).
  • Numeracy — Comparing quantities — which has more (EYLF 4).

VariationTurn the core activity into a short game with a silly or novel twist to keep motivation high.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Start with two-syllable words and the child's own name — the personal connection helps the concept land before generalising to other words.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

On persistence: if a child loses interest partway through a tricky cognitive task, invite one more playful attempt rather than letting the first try be the only one.

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: guessing or attempting an answer in front of others carries a small social risk — a relaxed tone protects a child's willingness to keep trying.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Support the Thinking, Not the Answer

Parents make the task slightly easier when needed, rather than supplying the answer directly.

🏠 Try This at Home

"Get your bag, put your shoes on, then come sit down." Break it into two plus a pause if it's too much.

Turn-Taking in a Rule-Based Game
SEL: Self-Management
▾
Why This Matters

Simple rule-based games (Duck Duck Goose, Musical Chairs) ask children to hold an impulse — wait your turn — while tracking a shared rule structure, a step up from the looser turn-taking practised earlier.

Activities
  • Duck Duck Goose (EYLF 2): Classic turn-taking with a clear, simple rule.
  • Simple board game (EYLF 2): Any game with a visible turn order (dice, spinner) builds the same waiting skill.

VariationLook for a natural moment during free play where this week's skill genuinely comes up, rather than only practising it in a set activity.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

If a child struggles to wait, give them a visible job during others' turns ("you're the counter — count how many steps!") so waiting isn't purely passive.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

Model the target behaviour yourself first — children copy what they see far more readily than what they're told.

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: this term has tracked a steady shift from needing support (Autonomy) toward attempting things independently in a social context (Initiative) — this week's skill sits squarely in that shift.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Notice and Name It

Parents notice and warmly name the target behaviour when it happens, rather than only correcting when it doesn't.

🏠 Try This at Home

Start with their own name — cla-p once per syllable together.

Managing Buttons and Zips
ADL: Fine Motor Dressing
▾
Why This Matters

Buttons and zips are two of the harder fasteners in the dressing sequence, requiring both fine motor control and a two-handed coordinated action.

Activities
  • Button-practice board (EYLF 3): A dressing frame or large buttons on fabric for low-pressure practice away from real clothes.
  • Zip starts (EYLF 3): Practise starting a zip (the hardest part) with an adult holding the fabric taut.

VariationBuild the practice into a real daily routine rather than a stand-alone drill — the transfer to real life matters most.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Large buttons and chunky zip pulls are far easier to learn on than small fasteners — practise on an oversized version before expecting it on real clothing.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent steps in too quickly: "Give it a little longer before helping — the attempt itself is the practice."

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: "I can do it myself" is the developmental hallmark of this stage — even when it's slower or messier than an adult stepping in.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Let Them Struggle Before You Help

Parents wait through a genuine struggle before stepping in, rather than helping out of habit.

🏠 Try This at Home

An old adult shirt with big buttons is much easier to learn on than small ones.
