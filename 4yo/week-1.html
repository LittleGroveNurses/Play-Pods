Week 1

Week 1 introduces 4-year-olds to reasoning language through "because" sentences, the step-hop coordination of an early skipping attempt, and the first building block of pre-literacy — rhyming. Social-emotional focus is on learning and using peers' names as the foundation for genuine cooperative play later in the term, while self-help targets the everyday school-readiness skill of opening a lunchbox independently. This week also introduces two recurring threads that run across the whole term: a graded single-leg balance challenge, and a rotating locomotor warm-up.

Gross Motor Pod
Skipping (Intro) + Balance + Locomotor Warm-Up
FMS: Skipping (Intro)
▾
Why This Matters

Skipping is one of the most complex locomotor patterns children learn — it combines a step and a hop into a single rhythmic action, requiring bilateral coordination most 4-year-olds are only just beginning to develop. Full mastery isn't typical until 5–6 years, so this week is about the attempt, not the result — many children will gallop instead, which is a genuine developmental stepping stone, not a failure. Alongside this, we introduce two threads that will run every week of the term: a single-leg balance challenge (starting with a momentary stand and building week by week) and a rotating locomotor warm-up (this week, walking backward), plus a fine-motor pre-writing check-in.

🏃 FMS Technique Cues — Skipping Attempt
Step forward on one foot, then hop on that same foot
Swap to the other foot and repeat
Arms swing naturally, opposite to the stepping leg
Slow it right down — speed comes later
Galloping counts as a genuine attempt this week

FMS evidence & branding note →

⚖️ Balance Challenge This Week

Stand on the preferred foot momentarily — even a wobbly one-second stand counts. This is week 1 of a graded challenge that builds across the term: by week 6 children will hold for 10 seconds on each foot, and by week 9 with eyes closed. Keep it light and game-like — "how still can you be, like a statue?"

🔄 Locomotor Warm-Up — Walking Backward

Two steps backward, with a hand held if needed. This rotates weekly (tiptoe walking, heel-to-toe, toe-to-heel) to build the range of controlled movement patterns 4-year-olds need beyond simple forward walking and running.

Activities
  • Skip or gallop attempt (EYLF 3): Model slowly, step-hop, step-hop. Most children will gallop (same foot always leads) rather than true skip (alternating) — both are welcome this week.
  • Balance challenge (EYLF 3): One brief timed stand per child, celebrated regardless of the count reached.
  • Backward walking warm-up (EYLF 3): Two steps backward along a line of tape, hand held if needed.
  • Fine motor — grasp check-in and vertical line (EYLF 4): Observe pencil grasp during a simple drawing task; model drawing a single vertical line top to bottom.

VariationTurn the skip/gallop attempt into a "follow the leader" line so children copy a peer rather than feeling individually watched.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Skipping progression: (1) march in place, lifting knees. (2) step-hop on one spot, same foot. (3) step-hop moving forward, same foot (this is a gallop). (4) alternate feet between step-hops (this is a true skip). Most 4-year-olds will sit between stages 2 and 3 this week — that's exactly on track.

Balance: if a child can't manage even a momentary stand, let them hold a wall or your hand lightly while attempting the lift — the attempt matters more than the independence at this stage.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent says "he can't skip": "Almost no 4-year-old has this fully — a gallop is a completely normal stage on the way there. Let's celebrate the attempt."

When a child avoids the balance challenge: "Make it a silly game rather than a test — 'let's see if you can be a flamingo for one whole second!' Distraction and play reduce the pressure."

On persistence: some children will want to stop after one wobble. Gently invite a second try — "one more go, then we'll move on" — rather than letting the first attempt be the only one; this builds the same staying-with-it muscle as everything else this term.

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: at 4, children are moving from "can I do this with support" (Autonomy) toward "can I attempt this hard new thing in front of others" (Initiative). A skipping attempt that's met with warmth rather than correction — even when it looks like a gallop — protects that willingness to try.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Effort Over Mastery

Parents learn to celebrate the attempt at a genuinely hard skill, rather than waiting for the "correct" version before offering praise.

🏠 Try This at Home

Practise the step-hop rhythm together — say "up, up" as you climb or step. Time a single-leg balance and celebrate any count at all. Walk backward two steps holding hands as a silly game before dinner.

Communication Pod
Complex Sentences with "Because"
OWL — Observe, Wait, Listen
▾
Why This Matters

Using "because" to connect an idea to a reason marks a genuine leap in reasoning language — it's the first step toward the complex, connected sentences 4-year-olds need for storytelling and later classroom explanation. This week also introduces two threads that rotate weekly across the term: a spatial-concept pair (this week, big/little) and expanded body-part vocabulary (this week, elbows, chest, shoulders) — both drawn from what a school-readiness language assessment actually checks for.

Activities
  • "Because" sentences (EYLF 5): Ask simple why-questions — "why do we wear a coat?" — and model the frame: "because it's cold." Accept any causal attempt, even an imperfect one.
  • Big/little sorting (EYLF 5): Sort toys or pictures into big and little groups, naming the concept each time.
  • Body parts — elbows, chest, shoulders (EYLF 5): Simon Says or a body-part song that names and points to each in turn.

Variation"Just Because" story game — one person starts a silly sentence, the next adds "because…", building a chain around the circle.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

If a child can't produce "because" independently, model the full frame and pause for them to fill in just the reason: "we wear a coat because it's… ?"

Body parts: point to your own first, then ask the child to point to theirs — imitation before independent recall.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a child gives an unrelated reason: "Any attempt at connecting an idea to a reason counts here — the logic will sharpen with practice. Respond warmly to the attempt itself."

When a parent answers for the child: "Try waiting the full five seconds after asking 'why' — the pause does more work than a quick prompt."

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: explaining your own thinking out loud, even imperfectly, is a small act of initiative. Children who are corrected too quickly on the logic of a "because" sentence can become reluctant to offer reasoning at all.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Ask "Why," Then Wait

Parents practise asking a genuine "why" question and holding the pause for a full five seconds before offering the answer themselves.

🏠 Try This at Home

Ask one "why" question a day — "why do we brush our teeth?" — and wait. Model the frame if needed: "because it keeps them clean." Point to elbows, chest, and shoulders in the bath or while getting dressed and name them together.

Cognitive Pod
Rhyming Awareness (Pre-Literacy)
Pre-Literacy: Phonological Awareness
▾
Why This Matters

Rhyming awareness is one of the earliest and best-established predictors of later reading success — noticing that "cat" and "hat" sound alike is a child's first real experience of playing with the sounds inside words, rather than just their meaning. This week also introduces two rotating threads that run through the whole term: pre-literacy/print conventions (this week, how a book works — front, back, and listening) and early numeracy (this week, counting with one-to-one correspondence).

🧠 Pre-Literacy Skill This Week

Rhyme detection (phonological awareness) + book/print conventions + counting with 1:1 correspondence

Activities
  • Rhyming pairs (EYLF 5): "Cat-hat," "dog-log" — say a word and ask the child to find or suggest a rhyme. Silly/nonsense rhymes are fine and often more fun.
  • Book conventions (EYLF 5): Show the front and back cover, ask "where do we start?" and model holding the book the right way up.
  • Counting with 1:1 correspondence (EYLF 4): Count out loud while touching each object once — five blocks, five steps, five claps — checking the child touches one object per number rather than rushing ahead.

VariationRhyming basket — collect small objects, sort into pairs that rhyme even if silly ("sock-block" — not a real rhyme pair, but still a good discussion starter about what does and doesn't rhyme).

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

If a child can't generate a rhyme, offer two choices — "does 'hat' rhyme with 'cat' or with 'dog'?" — recognition comes well before generation at this age.

For counting, physically move each object as it's counted rather than just pointing — the physical action anchors the one-to-one link more reliably than pointing alone.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a child loses interest partway through: that's a completely normal response to a genuinely tricky new skill — offer one more turn in a playful voice rather than pushing through a full set. Staying engaged through a little difficulty, even briefly, is worth naming and celebrating on its own.

When a parent supplies the rhyme immediately: "Try two options instead of the answer — 'is it hat or shoe?' — it keeps your child in the thinking seat."

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: rhyming games invite children to guess out loud in front of others, which is a small social risk. A relaxed, playful tone — where a "wrong" rhyme still gets a warm response — protects a child's willingness to keep guessing.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Play With Sounds, Not Just Words

Parents learn to notice and name rhyme in everyday language — in songs, names, and books — rather than treating it as a separate "lesson."

🏠 Try This at Home

Play "I spy a rhyme" in the car or bath — say a word and ask what rhymes with it, offering two choices if needed. Count out loud together at snack time, touching each item as you say the number.

Social-Emotional Pod
Learning and Using Peers' Names
SEL: Social Awareness + Relationship Skills
▾
Why This Matters

Being able to identify and address peers by name is the foundation everything else in the term's social-emotional progression builds on — you can't play a genuinely cooperative game with someone whose name you don't know. Week 1 also opens two routines that run every week from here on: a name-based circle-time ritual, and small "prosocial moments" woven into snack time and transitions (asking before borrowing, saying sorry for a bump) rather than taught as a separate topic.

Activities
  • Name song at circle time (EYLF 1): A simple call-and-response song using each child's name in turn.
  • Name toss (EYLF 2): Roll or toss a soft ball while saying the receiving child's name — a light, low-pressure way to practise names in both directions.
  • Photo name matching (EYLF 1): Simple photo cards of the group, matching name to face.

Variation"Guess who" — describe a feature ("who has the red shoes?") and let children guess the name.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Start with just the child's own name and one or two peers, expanding the circle across the week rather than expecting all names at once.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a child gets a name wrong: "Just model the correct name warmly — 'yes, that's Isla!' — no need to make it a correction moment."

Prosocial moment: if a child reaches for another child's toy at snack time, this is a natural chance to model "can I have a turn, please?" rather than a scripted lesson — the same goes for a gentle "sorry" if two children bump during a game.

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: using a peer's name to invite them into play is a genuine act of social initiative for a 4-year-old. Facilitators and parents can actively notice and name this — "you called Jack's name to play with you!" — to reinforce it.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Model Names, Don't Test Them

Parents use peer names naturally in conversation about the session, rather than quizzing their child on who's who.

🏠 Try This at Home

Talk about the group using names: "did you play with Sam today?" Keep it light and conversational rather than a memory test.

Self-Help Pod
Opening Own Lunchbox / Food Packaging
ADL: Feeding Independence
▾
Why This Matters

Managing a lunchbox — opening containers, unwrapping snacks — is one of the most common practical barriers families report at school entry. It's a genuinely fiddly fine-motor and problem-solving task, and it's easy for adults to open everything before a child even tries. This week also names a routine that will run every session from here: taking shoes and socks off and on during transitions is treated as a deliberate self-help moment, not just something that happens incidentally.

Activities
  • Open snack containers (EYLF 3): Practise with a range of closures — click-lock lids, zip-lock bags, wrapped items — letting the child try before offering help.
  • Shoes off/on at transitions (EYLF 3): Build this into arrival and departure every week — a natural, repeated opportunity rather than a taught topic.
  • Pretend lunchbox play (EYLF 1): Low-pressure practice packing and unpacking a play lunchbox before trying the real thing.

VariationTurn it into a "lunchbox detective" game — guess what's inside by the shape or weight before opening.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Start with the easiest closure type the child has, adding one harder closure at a time. Loosen a very tight lid slightly before handing it over so the first success comes quickly.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent opens everything before handing it over: "Let's hand it over closed and give her 30 seconds to try first — even a partial attempt is useful practice."

When a child gets frustrated: "Offer a hint, not a takeover — 'try turning it the other way' — before stepping in."

⚠️ Common Parent Trap

It's much faster to open everything in advance — but every container a 4-year-old opens themselves is practice for the independence they'll need at school, where no one will do it for them.

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: a child who insists "I can do it myself" with a stubborn lid is exercising exactly the initiative this stage is about — even when it's slower and messier than an adult stepping in.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Let Them Struggle Before You Help

Parents practise waiting through a genuine struggle with a container before stepping in, rather than pre-opening everything out of habit.

🏠 Try This at Home

Pack tomorrow's lunchbox together and let your child open one container themselves before you head out — even if it takes a few tries. Practise taking shoes off at the front door each day.
