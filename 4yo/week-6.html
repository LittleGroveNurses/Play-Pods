Week 6

Week 6 builds on the term's progression: hopping — alternating feet, endurance, and jumping backward in motor, using past and future tense in communication, counting with 1:1 correspondence (consolidation) in cognitive development, stranger and new-adult safety awareness in social-emotional growth, and managing a jacket independently in self-help. This week also continues the balance, locomotor warm-up, and letter/numeral threads running across the whole term.

Hopping — Alternating Feet, Endurance, and Jumping Backward
FMS: Hopping + Jumping Backward
▾
Why This Matters

This week brings together three related but distinct skills: alternating-foot hopping (a harder coordination task than single-foot hopping), single-foot hop endurance (building up to five consecutive hops on each foot separately), and jumping backward — none of which had been explicitly targeted before this term.

🏃 FMS Technique Cues — Hopping — Alternating Feet, Endurance, and Jumping Backward
For alternating hops: hop-hop on one foot, then swap
For endurance: try to get to five hops before your foot touches down properly
For jumping backward: small hops, look over your shoulder first
Bend your knees to land, every time

FMS evidence & branding note →

⚖️ Balance Challenge This Week

Stand on the preferred foot for 10 seconds.

🔄 Locomotor Warm-Up

Heel-to-toe forward, 5 steps.

Activities
  • Alternating-foot hopping (EYLF 3): Hop twice on one foot, then swap to the other, building a rhythm.
  • Single-foot hop endurance (EYLF 3): Build toward five consecutive hops on the preferred foot, then the other — track progress as a personal best.
  • Jumping backward (EYLF 3): Start with one backward jump, building toward two consecutive jumps.
  • Balance challenge (EYLF 3): Stand on the preferred foot for 10 seconds.
  • Locomotor warm-up (EYLF 3): Heel-to-toe forward, 5 steps.

VariationVary the activity to keep engagement high while practising the same underlying skill.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

These are three separate skills bundled into one week — it's fine to spend more time on whichever one a child finds hardest rather than giving each equal time.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent worries about pace: "Every child develops these skills at a different rate — what matters is regular, playful practice."

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: attempting a genuinely hard physical skill in front of peers, even imperfectly, is an act of initiative worth naming and celebrating.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Effort Over Mastery

Parents celebrate the attempt at a hard skill, not just a perfect result.

🏠 Try This at Home

Alternating-foot hops, single-foot hop endurance, and small jumps backward — whichever's hardest gets the most practice.

Using Past and Future Tense
▾
Why This Matters

Correctly using past tense ("we went") and future tense ("we will go") reflects a child's growing internal sense of time — a genuine grammatical milestone that also supports this week's cognitive time-concept focus.

The Very Impatient Caterpillar — Ross Burach

Activities
  • "What did we do?" recap (EYLF 5): At the end of an activity, ask the child to describe it in past tense.
  • Spatial concept — bottom/top (EYLF 5): Practical positioning practice.
  • Object use — book, scissors, fridge (EYLF 5): "What do we do with scissors?"

VariationBring the target language into an existing favourite activity rather than treating it as a stand-alone drill.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

If a child overregularises irregular past tense ("we goed" instead of "we went"), that's a sign of active grammar-rule learning, not an error to correct directly — simply model the correct form back.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent answers for the child: "Try waiting five seconds before stepping in — the pause does more work than a prompt."

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: offering language, even imperfectly, is a small act of communicative initiative — respond warmly to the attempt.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Model, Then Wait

Parents offer the language model, then give real waiting time before jumping in.

🏠 Try This at Home

"What did we do yesterday? What will we do tomorrow?" anchored to real events.

Counting with 1:1 Correspondence (Consolidation)
Pre-Literacy: Name 10 more letters
▾
Why This Matters

This week consolidates the one-to-one counting introduced in Week 1, checking that children reliably touch one object per number said, rather than rushing ahead or double-counting — a genuine numeracy foundation, not just rote number recitation.

🧠 Pre-Literacy / Numeracy Threads This Week

Pre-Literacy: Name 10 more letters + Numeracy: Missing-numeral in a sequence

Activities
  • Counting consolidation (EYLF 4): Count larger sets (up to 10) with physical movement of each object.
  • Pre-literacy — Name 10 more uppercase letters (EYLF 5).
  • Numeracy — Missing-numeral in a sequence (EYLF 4).

VariationTurn the core activity into a short game with a silly or novel twist to keep motivation high.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

If a child can recite numbers well beyond their reliable 1:1 counting, that's a very common mismatch — the counting sequence (rote) and the counting principle (1:1 correspondence) develop somewhat separately.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

On persistence: if a child loses interest partway through a tricky cognitive task, invite one more playful attempt rather than letting the first try be the only one.

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: guessing or attempting an answer in front of others carries a small social risk — a relaxed tone protects a child's willingness to keep trying.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Support the Thinking, Not the Answer

Parents make the task slightly easier when needed, rather than supplying the answer directly.

🏠 Try This at Home

Keep it reassuring, not scary — a simple "check with someone you know" rule is enough.

Stranger and New-Adult Safety Awareness
SEL: Safety Awareness
▾
Why This Matters

As 4-year-olds gain independence and confidence, age-appropriate safety awareness around unfamiliar adults becomes a genuinely important, practical social-emotional skill — introduced gently, without frightening the child.

Activities
  • "Who's a safe grown-up?" discussion (EYLF 2): Simple, reassuring conversation about known trusted adults versus strangers.
  • Role-play scenarios (EYLF 2): Gentle, low-pressure practice of what to do if approached by someone unfamiliar — always framed calmly, never as a scare tactic.

VariationLook for a natural moment during free play where this week's skill genuinely comes up, rather than only practising it in a set activity.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Keep the tone matter-of-fact and reassuring throughout — the goal is calm awareness, not anxiety. Avoid detailed hypothetical danger scenarios; a simple "check with a grown-up you know" rule is enough at this age.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

Model the target behaviour yourself first — children copy what they see far more readily than what they're told.

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: this term has tracked a steady shift from needing support (Autonomy) toward attempting things independently in a social context (Initiative) — this week's skill sits squarely in that shift.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Notice and Name It

Parents notice and warmly name the target behaviour when it happens, rather than only correcting when it doesn't.

🏠 Try This at Home

Self-Help Pod
Managing a Jacket Independently
ADL: Dressing Independence
▾
Why This Matters

A jacket — the "flip trick" for putting it on, managing a zip, and taking it off — is one of the more complex dressing items and a genuinely common source of frustration for 4-year-olds.

Activities
  • The flip trick (EYLF 3): Lay the jacket open on the floor, arms in from the top, then flip overhead — a well-known technique that suits this age.
  • Zip practice (EYLF 3): Practise starting and finishing a zip independently.

VariationBuild the practice into a real daily routine rather than a stand-alone drill — the transfer to real life matters most.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

The flip trick genuinely works better than the "arms in one sleeve at a time" method for many 4-year-olds — teach it explicitly rather than assuming they'll discover it.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent steps in too quickly: "Give it a little longer before helping — the attempt itself is the practice."

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: "I can do it myself" is the developmental hallmark of this stage — even when it's slower or messier than an adult stepping in.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Let Them Struggle Before You Help

Parents wait through a genuine struggle before stepping in, rather than helping out of habit.
