Week 7

Week 7 builds on the term's progression: scissor cutting along a line in motor, descriptive language and comparing objects in communication, memory — sequential recall in cognitive development, conflict resolution with adult support in social-emotional growth, and preparing a simple snack in self-help. This week also continues the balance, locomotor warm-up, and letter/numeral threads running across the whole term.

Scissor Cutting Along a Line
FMS: Scissor Skills
▾
Why This Matters

Scissor cutting is a genuine fine-motor and bilateral-coordination milestone — one hand cuts while the other holds and turns the paper — and following a straight line adds a visual-motor precision component on top.

🏃 FMS Technique Cues — Scissor Cutting Along a Line
Thumb up, scissors open wide
Small, controlled snips, not one big cut
Turn the paper with your other hand, not the scissors
Follow the line with your eyes as you cut

FMS evidence & branding note →

⚖️ Balance Challenge This Week

Stand on the other foot for 10 seconds.

🔄 Locomotor Warm-Up

Toe-to-heel backward, 4 steps.

Activities
  • Straight-line cutting (EYLF 4): Thick, dark lines on sturdy paper or card, cut with child-safe scissors.
  • Fringe cutting (EYLF 4): Snipping fringes into paper — an easier warm-up before a full straight-line cut.
  • Balance challenge (EYLF 3): Stand on the other foot for 10 seconds.
  • Locomotor warm-up (EYLF 3): Toe-to-heel backward, 4 steps.

VariationVary the activity to keep engagement high while practising the same underlying skill.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

If a child struggles to coordinate both hands, focus first on just opening and closing the scissors with one snip at a time before expecting continuous cutting along a line.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent worries about pace: "Every child develops these skills at a different rate — what matters is regular, playful practice."

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: attempting a genuinely hard physical skill in front of peers, even imperfectly, is an act of initiative worth naming and celebrating.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Effort Over Mastery

Parents celebrate the attempt at a hard skill, not just a perfect result.

🏠 Try This at Home

Thick, dark lines on sturdy paper. Snipping fringes first is a good warm-up.

Descriptive Language and Comparing Objects
▾
Why This Matters

Using descriptive adjectives (rough, shiny, heavy) and making comparisons between objects ("this one's bigger than that one") builds the rich, specific vocabulary that supports both storytelling and later comprehension of comparative concepts in maths.

📚 Book of the Week

Alexander and the Terrible, Horrible, No Good, Very Bad Day — Judith Viorst

Rich, funny descriptive language throughout — a strong model for this week's comparing and describing focus.

More book strategies at Hanen Book Nook →

Activities
  • Describe the object (EYLF 5): A mystery bag of textured objects, describing each by feel before naming it.
  • Spatial concept — low/high (EYLF 5): Practical positioning practice.
  • Body parts — neck, back, stomach (EYLF 5): Simon Says or a body-part song.

VariationBring the target language into an existing favourite activity rather than treating it as a stand-alone drill.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Offer a word bank of a few describing words at first (soft, hard, bumpy, smooth) — generating novel adjectives unprompted is genuinely hard even for adults.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent answers for the child: "Try waiting five seconds before stepping in — the pause does more work than a prompt."

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: offering language, even imperfectly, is a small act of communicative initiative — respond warmly to the attempt.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Model, Then Wait

Parents offer the language model, then give real waiting time before jumping in.

A mystery bag of textured things — describe it before you look.

Cognitive Pod
Memory — Sequential Recall
Pre-Literacy: Story retelling / book comprehension
▾
Why This Matters

Recalling a short sequence in the correct order (three items, three instructions, three story events) is a working-memory skill that underpins following multi-step directions and later academic tasks.

🧠 Pre-Literacy / Numeracy Threads This Week

Pre-Literacy: Story retelling / book comprehension + Numeracy: Reading numerals to 50

Activities
  • Sequential recall game (EYLF 4): "I went to the shop and bought…" — each round adds one more item to remember in order.
  • Pre-literacy — Story retelling — book comprehension (EYLF 5).
  • Numeracy — Reading numerals to 50 (EYLF 4).

VariationTurn the core activity into a short game with a silly or novel twist to keep motivation high.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Start with two items before building to three — the jump from two to three held items in sequence is a genuinely large one at this age.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

On persistence: if a child loses interest partway through a tricky cognitive task, invite one more playful attempt rather than letting the first try be the only one.

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: guessing or attempting an answer in front of others carries a small social risk — a relaxed tone protects a child's willingness to keep trying.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Support the Thinking, Not the Answer

Parents make the task slightly easier when needed, rather than supplying the answer directly.

🏠 Try This at Home

"I went to the shop and bought..." — add one more item each round.

Equipment
Letter and numeral cards

Social-Emotional Pod
Conflict Resolution with Adult Support
SEL: Relationship Skills
▾
Why This Matters

Peer conflict (over a toy, a turn, a space) is completely normal at this age — the skill being built isn't avoiding conflict, but navigating it with growing independence while an adult stays nearby to support rather than solve it outright.

Activities
  • Guided negotiation (EYLF 2): When a conflict arises, ask both children what happened and what they'd like to happen next, rather than issuing a verdict.
  • Apology modelling (EYLF 2): Model "I'm sorry" or "excuse me" naturally when a genuine accident or bump occurs.

VariationLook for a natural moment during free play where this week's skill genuinely comes up, rather than only practising it in a set activity.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Resist solving the conflict outright — ask each child what they think would be fair first. Step in with a suggestion only if they genuinely can't find their own way through.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

Model the target behaviour yourself first — children copy what they see far more readily than what they're told.

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: this term has tracked a steady shift from needing support (Autonomy) toward attempting things independently in a social context (Initiative) — this week's skill sits squarely in that shift.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Notice and Name It

Parents notice and warmly name the target behaviour when it happens, rather than only correcting when it doesn't.

Self-Help Pod
Preparing a Simple Snack
ADL: Food Preparation
▾
Why This Matters

Simple, safe food preparation (spreading, assembling) builds both fine motor skill and a genuine sense of competence and contribution — a task 4-year-olds can meaningfully do themselves rather than just watch.

Activities
  • Spread and assemble (EYLF 3): A simple snack like crackers with spread and toppings, prepared start to finish by the child with supervision.
  • Pouring practice (EYLF 3): Pouring a small amount of liquid from a small jug into a cup.

VariationBuild the practice into a real daily routine rather than a stand-alone drill — the transfer to real life matters most.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Expect spills, and treat them matter-of-factly with a cloth handed to the child to help clean up — this keeps the activity feeling successful rather than like a failure.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent steps in too quickly: "Give it a little longer before helping — the attempt itself is the practice."

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: "I can do it myself" is the developmental hallmark of this stage — even when it's slower or messier than an adult stepping in.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Let Them Struggle Before You Help

Parents wait through a genuine struggle before stepping in, rather than helping out of habit.

Model "I'm sorry" or "excuse me" naturally when a real bump or accident happens.
