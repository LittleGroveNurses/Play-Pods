Week 4

Week 4 builds on the term's progression: stairs — alternating feet in motor, story retell — beginning, middle, end in communication, letter recognition — own name in cognitive development, waiting and managing frustration in a queue in social-emotional growth, and using cutlery competently in self-help. This week also continues the balance, locomotor warm-up, and letter/numeral threads running across the whole term.

Gross Motor Pod
Stairs — Alternating Feet
FMS: Stairs — Alternating Feet
▾
Why This Matters

Managing stairs with alternating feet, holding only a rail rather than an adult's hand, is a genuine motor milestone that hasn't been explicitly targeted in the program before now — most 4-year-olds are right at this transition point.

🏃 FMS Technique Cues — Stairs — Alternating Feet
One hand lightly on the rail, not gripping tightly
Alternate feet — one foot per step, not two feet together
Look at your feet, then look ahead
Take your time — there's no rush

FMS evidence & branding note →

⚖️ Balance Challenge This Week

Stand on the preferred foot for 5 seconds.

🔄 Locomotor Warm-Up

Walk backward, 4 steps.

Activities
  • Stairs, alternating feet, going up (EYLF 3): Practise with a rail only, no hand-holding, on a real or practice staircase.
  • Stairs, alternating feet, going down (EYLF 3): The harder direction — often needs more practice than going up.
  • Balance challenge (EYLF 3): Stand on the preferred foot for 5 seconds.
  • Locomotor warm-up (EYLF 3): Walk backward, 4 steps.

VariationVary the activity to keep engagement high while practising the same underlying skill.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Going down is harder than going up — if a child manages alternating feet going up but reverts to both-feet-together going down, that's completely typical; keep practising both directions separately.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent worries about pace: "Every child develops these skills at a different rate — what matters is regular, playful practice."

⚠️ Common Parent Trap

It's tempting to hold a child's hand the whole way for safety — but a rail-only approach, closely supervised, is what actually builds the independent skill. Stay close rather than holding on.

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: attempting a genuinely hard physical skill in front of peers, even imperfectly, is an act of initiative worth naming and celebrating.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Effort Over Mastery

Parents celebrate the attempt at a hard skill, not just a perfect result.

🏠 Try This at Home

Stay close for safety, but let them manage without holding your hand.

Communication Pod
Story Retell — Beginning, Middle, End
▾
Why This Matters

Explicitly retelling a story with a clear beginning, middle, and end is a genuine step up from simply sequencing events — it's a benchmark language-development skill, and this week names the structure directly rather than leaving it implicit.

📚 Book of the Week

The Three Billy Goats Gruff — traditional, illus. various

A clean beginning-middle-end structure with repetition, ideal for practising story retell in order.

More book strategies at Hanen Book Nook →

Activities
  • Retell this week's book (EYLF 5): After reading, ask "what happened first? Then what? How did it end?"
  • Spatial concept — close/open (EYLF 5): Practical practice with containers, doors, books.
  • Object use — stove, coat, pencil (EYLF 5): "What is a stove for?"

VariationBring the target language into an existing favourite activity rather than treating it as a stand-alone drill.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Use the words "first," "then," and "at the end" explicitly as scaffolding language — over the term, children will start using these connectors unprompted.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent answers for the child: "Try waiting five seconds before stepping in — the pause does more work than a prompt."

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: offering language, even imperfectly, is a small act of communicative initiative — respond warmly to the attempt.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Model, Then Wait

Parents offer the language model, then give real waiting time before jumping in.

🏠 Try This at Home

"What happened first? Then what? How did it end?" Use those exact words as prompts.

Cognitive Pod
Letter Recognition — Own Name
Pre-Literacy: Print direction, left-to-right
▾
Why This Matters

Recognising the letters in one's own name is usually a 4-year-old's entry point into the alphabet — it's personally meaningful and motivating in a way abstract letter drills aren't.

🧠 Pre-Literacy / Numeracy Threads This Week

Pre-Literacy: Print direction, left-to-right + Numeracy: Matching a numeral to a quantity

Activities
  • Name recognition (EYLF 5): Point out the child's name written down; can they find the matching letters elsewhere?
  • Pre-literacy — Print direction: left-to-right, top-to-bottom (EYLF 5).
  • Numeracy — Matching a numeral to a quantity (EYLF 4).

VariationTurn the core activity into a short game with a silly or novel twist to keep motivation high.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Start with just the first letter of the name before expecting recognition of the whole word — most children get this letter first and fastest.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

On persistence: if a child loses interest partway through a tricky cognitive task, invite one more playful attempt rather than letting the first try be the only one.

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: guessing or attempting an answer in front of others carries a small social risk — a relaxed tone protects a child's willingness to keep trying.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Support the Thinking, Not the Answer

Parents make the task slightly easier when needed, rather than supplying the answer directly.

🏠 Try This at Home

Point out the first letter and see if they can spot it elsewhere, like on a cereal box.

Social-Emotional Pod
Waiting and Managing Frustration in a Queue
SEL: Self-Management
▾
Why This Matters

Queuing — for a turn on equipment, for a drink, for a game — is one of the most common frustration triggers for 4-year-olds, and managing it calmly is a genuine emotional-regulation skill worth naming and practising directly.

Activities
  • Real queuing practice (EYLF 2): Any equipment with limited spots creates a natural, low-stakes queuing opportunity.
  • Naming the wait (EYLF 5): "You're waiting for your turn — that's tricky. Two more people, then it's you."

VariationLook for a natural moment during free play where this week's skill genuinely comes up, rather than only practising it in a set activity.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

Give a concrete marker of how long the wait is ("two more turns") rather than an abstract one ("soon") — 4-year-olds manage waiting much better with something countable.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

Model the target behaviour yourself first — children copy what they see far more readily than what they're told.

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: this term has tracked a steady shift from needing support (Autonomy) toward attempting things independently in a social context (Initiative) — this week's skill sits squarely in that shift.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Notice and Name It

Parents notice and warmly name the target behaviour when it happens, rather than only correcting when it doesn't.

🏠 Try This at Home

"Two more turns, then it's you" works much better than "soon."

Self-Help Pod
Using Cutlery Competently
ADL: Mealtime Independence
▾
Why This Matters

Competent, independent cutlery use (not just holding a spoon, but managing a fork and starting to use a knife for spreading) is an expected school-readiness self-help skill by 4.

Activities
  • Practice mealtime (EYLF 3): Use real cutlery with age-appropriate foods, resisting the urge to intervene at the first spill.
  • Spreading practice (EYLF 3): A butter knife and soft spread for early knife-use practice.

VariationBuild the practice into a real daily routine rather than a stand-alone drill — the transfer to real life matters most.

🪜 Scaffolding This Activity

If a child reverts to fingers when frustrated, that's normal under fatigue — gently redirect to cutlery rather than treating it as a behaviour issue.

🎯 Facilitator Coaching Cues

When a parent steps in too quickly: "Give it a little longer before helping — the attempt itself is the practice."

🌱 Development Note (Erikson)

Initiative vs Guilt: "I can do it myself" is the developmental hallmark of this stage — even when it's slower or messier than an adult stepping in.

👪 Parent Coaching Focus: Let Them Struggle Before You Help

Parents wait through a genuine struggle before stepping in, rather than helping out of habit.

🏠 Try This at Home

Resist stepping in at the first spill — it's part of learning.
